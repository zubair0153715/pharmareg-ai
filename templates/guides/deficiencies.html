7 labeling deficiencies that delay SFDA & EDE approvals

By Zubair Ahmed, Pharm-D · PharmaReg AI

Most reviewer queries on pharmaceutical product labels trace back to a small set of repeat defects. These are the seven we flag most often in audits for the Gulf market — each one fixable before submission.

1. Vague storage conditions

The defect: "Store at room temperature" with no explicit temperature or humidity bounds.

Why reviewers flag it: Assessors expect storage statements tied directly to the product's stability data. Vague claims almost always draw a query asking for the exact conditions.

The fix: "Store at 25 °C ± 2 °C / 60% ± 5% RH. Excursions permitted to 15–30 °C. Do not freeze." — adapted to the actual stability data and climatic zone.

2. Boxed warning not matching the reference product

The defect: The generic's boxed warning is paraphrased, reworded, or given different prominence than the originator's.

Why reviewers flag it: Assessors check generic labeling against the reference product line-by-line. Any divergence in safety wording is treated as a potential safety gap.

The fix: Carry the boxed warning over verbatim from the reference/originator labeling, with identical wording, prominence, and placement.

3. Undeclared excipients of concern

The defect: Excipients such as lactose or parabens appear in the composition but are not declared in the warnings section.

Why reviewers flag it: Patient-safety labeling. Excipient warnings are routinely checked across SFDA, EDE, FDA, and MHRA reviews alike.

The fix: Declare every excipient of concern in the warnings, cross-checked against the full composition — not just the headline ingredients.

4. Patient leaflet readability failures

The defect: Long, technical sentences in patient-facing sections; information ordered for regulators rather than patients.

Why reviewers flag it: Patient Information Leaflets carry readability and user-testing expectations. Leaflets patients can't follow fail their purpose — and the assessment.

The fix: Short sentences, plain terms for patient sections, and the standard question-and-answer order patients expect.

5. Incomplete marketing authorization holder details

The defect: MAH name or address incomplete — missing P.O. box, city, or local agent details required for the market.

Why reviewers flag it: An administrative deficiency, but one of the most common. It costs a query round every time.

The fix: Verify the MAH block against the target market's requirements before every submission — it differs between SFDA, EDE, FDA, and MHRA filings.

6. Dosage section missing titration or maximum dose

The defect: The maximum daily dose is stated but the titration schedule — or the maximum — from the reference label is missing.

Why reviewers flag it: Incomplete dosing information is a direct patient-safety issue and triggers assessment questions without exception.

The fix: Mirror the reference product's full dosage and administration section, including titration steps and the absolute maximum dose.

7. Label and leaflet disagreeing with each other

The defect: The outer carton, container label, and patient leaflet give slightly different information — different warnings, different storage wording, different excipient lists.

Why reviewers flag it: Reviewers read the label set as one package. Internal contradictions suggest the dossier wasn't controlled — and invite deeper scrutiny of everything else.

The fix: Harmonize all three documents from a single source of truth before submission, then re-check after every variation.
